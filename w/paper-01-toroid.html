The Toroid

the inductor, ring-form · one paper, one component · what · when · how · where it lives

First in a set — one component per paper. The toroid earns the opening slot because it isn't just a component, it's the birthplace of the whole field: the apparatus electromagnetic induction was discovered on was a ring exactly like this one. What it does in a circuit: store energy in a magnetic field and fight any change in current — the one component whose entire personality is resisting change.

What it is

A coil of wire wound on a ring-shaped core (usually ferrite or powdered iron). Electrically it's an inductor; wind a second coil on the same ring and it's a transformer. The ring shape is the point: it gives the magnetic field a closed loop to live in, so the flux stays inside the core instead of leaking out — which means high efficiency and very little radiated interference. That's why toroids show up wherever leakage would be a problem: power supplies, EMI filters, audio transformers.

the personality: a resistor drops voltage; a capacitor stores charge; the toroid stores current as a field, and resists changing it.

When & where it was discovered

Michael Faraday, 29 August 1831, at the Royal Institution in London. Faraday wound two separate coils onto opposite sides of an iron ring — his "induction ring" — connected one to a battery and the other to a galvanometer, and noticed that at the instant he closed or opened the battery circuit, the needle on the other coil kicked. Not while current flowed — only when it changed. That experiment discovered electromagnetic induction, and the device was, physically, a toroid. It was also the first transformer. So this component isn't just old — the ring is the exact geometry on which the relationship between electricity and magnetism was first caught in the act. Joseph Henry independently found self-induction around the same time (1831–32); the unit of inductance, the henry (H), is his.

How it works — turn the current, watch the voltage

Current in the winding makes a magnetic field (Ampère); the ring confines it. Then Faraday's rule does the rest: a changing field induces a voltage that opposes the change. Drag the slider to change the current and watch the induced voltage respond — steady current gives zero volts; fast change gives a big spike. The inductor is blind to current itself and reacts only to its rate of change.

An example in a circuit

The joule thief (the previous walkthrough) is the toroid showing off: interrupt ~50 mA through ~100 µH in about a microsecond and V = L·di/dt ≈ 5 V — a spike several times the 1.5 V battery, enough to light a blue LED the cell could never drive directly. Same trick, made continuous, is a boost converter in every phone charger. Wind two windings and reverse one and you have a common-mode choke — an EMI filter that passes your signal but blocks noise riding on both wires, because the ring's closed flux path cancels it. Every one of those is the same fact: a collapsing field makes a voltage, and a closed ring makes the field efficient.

Why the ring goes first. Every other component in this set will be introduced against the toroid, because the toroid is where the physics that powers all of them was first seen — a needle kicking the instant a current changed, on an iron ring in a London basement in 1831. What it does is almost philosophically simple: it refuses to let current change without a fight, and stores the argument as a magnetic field it can hand back later as a spike. A resistor forgets everything; a capacitor remembers voltage; the toroid remembers motion — how much current was flowing, and how hard you tried to stop it.
