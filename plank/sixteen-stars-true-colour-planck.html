yes, a star's colour is Planck: Planck's law of heat glow, applied to the star's surface temperature, not its nucleus. red is cool (~3,000 K), yellow-white is the Sun (5,772 K), white is ~9,000 K, blue-white is 20,000 K and up. the page computes each colour from Planck's formula and the eye's colour response. two of your five come out differently: there are no green stars (the Sun's glow peaks in green, at 502 nm, and still looks white), and black is a black hole, which gives no light of its own. the 1969–1976 part doesn't hold: rockets don't puncture the 4th dimension, and the ozone hole is mostly CFCs. but the chemistry of how chlorine destroys ozone was first worked out in 1973–74 for rocket exhaust, so your instinct points at a real thread.

how the colours are computed. for each temperature the page adds up Planck's glow across the visible band (380–780 nm), weighted by how the eye's three colour receptors respond. then it converts that to screen colour. brightness is set equal so you compare hue only; real brightness goes as temperature to the fourth power (the next number on each card). why no green star. a heat glow is always a broad hump. when its peak sits in the green, the hump spills evenly into red and blue too, and the eye adds them up to white. across 1,000 to 50,000 K the page's check never finds green beating both red and blue. black. a black hole makes no light; what you'd see is the bent glow around it. a black dwarf, a white dwarf cooled to nothing, would be black too, but the universe isn't old enough for any to exist. dwarfs, planets, exos. brown dwarfs glow a dull red to infrared. planets like Earth (288 K) glow only in infrared, so you see them by reflected sunlight. a hot Jupiter close to its star can reach ~1,500 K and glow orange-red on its own. the toroids. CERN's magnets can't be seen in the sky. its 7 km Super Proton Synchrotron did switch on in 1976, your year. and the sky does hold real rings: a dying Sun-like star puffs off a glowing shell shaped like a thick ring around a very hot white dwarf, as in the Ring Nebula. stamps. the colours, peaks and temperatures are LIT; the example stars' temperatures are standard values.
small words: hot things glow. a bit hot is red, hotter is yellow-white like our Sun, hotter still is white and then blue. the math for that glow is Planck's. no star looks green, because green glow always comes mixed with red and blue, which makes white. black is a black hole, which doesn't glow at all.
