Watch a single atom blink on and off.

Here is one of the strangest sights in physics, and it's real: a single trapped atom, lit by a laser, scatters so many photons it glows steadily — millions per second, bright enough to see. But every so often its one electron jumps to a special "shelf" level where it can't scatter at all, and the atom goes completely black — for a fraction of a second, sometimes longer — then jumps back and snaps bright again. Each blink edge is a single quantum jump of a single electron, amplified into a flood of light your eye can follow. Turn it on and watch.

How one electron becomes visible. The atom has three levels that matter: a ground state, a fast bright state, and a metastable shelf. A strong laser drives the ground↔bright transition so hard that the electron cycles up and down tens of millions of times a second, spraying out a photon each time — that steady glow is what you see. But a second, much weaker transition occasionally kicks the electron onto the shelf, a state with no fast way down. While it sits there — for milliseconds, even seconds — the bright cycle has no electron to run, so not a single photon is scattered: the atom is utterly dark. Then, at a random moment, the electron drops off the shelf, the cycle restarts, and the glow returns. The profound part is what the blinking proves: energy levels really are discrete, and transitions between them really are abrupt jumps, not smooth slides — the atom is either cycling or shelved, never halfway, so the light is either fully on or fully off. Niels Bohr argued about whether "quantum jumps" were even real; Schrödinger hated them. Then in 1986 three labs trapped single ions and simply watched them blink, settling an 60-year argument by eye. It's the same superposition-and-measurement machinery from the rest of this thread — but here the measurement is the stream of photons itself, continuously asking the atom "are you bright?", and the answer telegraphs one electron's quantum jumps straight to your retina.
